# An AI Bubble? National Strategy After the Boom

[English](README.md) | [עברית](README.he.md)

This repository is the research companion to [An AI Bubble? Why National Strategy Must Plan for What Comes After the Boom](https://mosheuziel.com/insights/ai-bubble-national-strategy).

The article does not predict that an AI bubble exists or attempt to time a correction. It asks a narrower policy question: if technological investment waves can move from rapid expansion to correction and later diffusion, what should a national AI strategy examine before that possibility materialises?

## The analytical path

The research is organised around four questions:

1. How can a valuable technology generate excessive valuations and later correction?
2. When does a market correction remain an investor loss, and when can it spread into a national economic crisis?
3. Which forms of resilience have historically reduced the transmission of financial shocks, and what did they cost during normal periods?
4. Which capabilities allow innovation and technology adoption to continue after a downturn?

## What this repository adds

The published article tells the argument as a readable narrative. This repository exposes the research structure behind it:

- the role assigned to each source
- the distinction between theoretical models, empirical studies and institutional reports
- an assessment of source quality
- an assessment of how directly each source supports the claim for which it is used
- the limits of applying evidence from housing, banking and earlier technological waves to AI

See the complete [source assessment](research/source-assessment.md).

## How to read the ratings

Two ratings are kept separate:

- **Source quality** assesses the publication venue, authorship, method and institutional authority.
- **Claim fit** assesses how directly the source supports the specific use made of it in the article.

A strong study can receive a lower claim-fit rating when its evidence comes from a different market or historical setting. Citation counts are considered as context, not as a standalone measure of quality.

## Current conclusion

The literature supports treating a sharp correction as a plausible scenario, not as a forecast. The policy question is therefore not whether governments can identify or prevent an AI bubble. It is whether they understand where financial and operational fragility is accumulating, what resilience would cost, and which capabilities should remain available for the next stage of adoption.
